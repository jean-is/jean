Accessibility

Last updated 1 November 2026

We, Warehouse SAS, mean Jean to be usable by everyone, whatever their sight, hearing, movement, language or script. This page says how far it is, what isn’t there yet, and how to tell us when something stops you.

Where it stands

jean.is is partially conformant with RGAA 4.1.2, France’s accessibility framework, because of the problems listed below.

What the audit found

Our audit of 1 November 2026 found that 55.7% of the criteria that apply are met: 49 of 88. Of the other 39, 36 aren’t met, and 3 couldn’t be checked, so they don’t count as met. The other 18 of RGAA’s 106 criteria don’t apply here. Partially conformant means at least half of them are met, and not all. We aren’t far over that line.

This is our own audit, read by AI and by no person yet. An AI made it. Separate AI readers, given none of its verdicts and told of no fix, then judged the criteria again in three rounds, and we count their verdicts. The last round read what our fixes claimed to close, and failed about half of it. No person has checked the result or taken Jean through it with a screen reader, and no outside party has audited Jean. You can read the full grid, criterion by criterion and page by page.

What this page covers

Jean at jean.is and the pages of this site, with the documents and collections you create in Jean: their screens and menus, Jean’s changes to accept or reject, and the files they are saved as. We audited them in French, the main pages in English too, and the homepage in Arabic. The desktop app shows the same pages but wasn’t audited on its own, and neither were the other languages. This page doesn’t cover what others make and Jean shows: sites opened in its browser, pages an AI writes in an answer, and the documents you bring. What you write in a document or a collection is yours, its headings and descriptions included.

What isn’t accessible yet

  • Hearing what happens: no person has yet checked what a screen reader says as you move through tasks, search, menus, panes, documents, collections, charts and scores. On the key form, the note that a key is free under 250 people isn’t announced, and shows far below the field that brings it up. Suggestions are announced politely, where RGAA’s test asks for an alert. The families page’s drawings hold words found nowhere else on the page, such as the subjects a parent can choose, and they are hidden from screen readers.
  • Losing your place: on a phone or in a narrow window, what Jean opens covers the task, unless you are writing in the message box. In a collection, choosing a kind for a new column moves the keyboard to its name at once. Tab can land where you can’t see it: behind a document’s pane on a new profile, behind the tour on the Excel page, and behind the message box in a long answer.
  • Finding your way: in the app, the tasks in the drawer are the only way around until search is found, after ten tasks. The lock screen has no way to the site map. Under the homepage’s first screen, the content isn’t marked as the main content, and on the app’s first screen the legal links aren’t marked as the page’s foot. Search has no landmark. Menus of links and buttons aren’t marked as lists. A comparison’s card and a design’s title look like headings and aren’t marked as such.
  • Keyboard and pointer: moving a pane or a collection’s column takes a drag, with no key to do it, and setting a pane’s width takes a drag or the arrow keys. A document shows the keyboard only by its caret and a thin line under its pane’s head. On comparison pages, a source’s tip covers the words around it, ignores Escape and goes if you point at it. A chart’s tooltip ignores Escape too.
  • Seeing it: search’s active result, the active entry in a document’s / menu and its pressed format buttons barely stand out. A link in a document’s selected block falls short of AA while its menu is open. A collection’s Filter, Sort and Settings show they are on by colour alone, a changed cell differs from its row by a tint, and search’s words found by a background. A collection’s lines and tint are stronger if your system asks for more contrast. Settings’ Capabilities shows what you have found only by the style of an icon.
  • Zoom and spacing: with more space between letters and lines, a design’s words overlap, and zooming the browser makes them smaller on screen rather than larger. At 320 by 256 pixels, a collection with changes to review leaves no room for its table.
  • Forms: many fields are labelled in ways RGAA’s test doesn’t list, and a few, such as a collection’s filter, by a hidden name alone. The message and search boxes show what they are for only as a hint, which goes as you type, and a tip for whoever points at them. A wrong value in a collection’s cell empties it with a brief message and no example, and a wrong recovery code doesn’t say what form it takes. The key form takes an address that doesn’t start with https, then leaves for a bare line in English that names no field. Deleting a creation gives a few seconds to undo without saying how long, and deleting a column or rejecting all changes acts at once. Search’s results and the box’s menu group their options in a way RGAA’s note doesn’t accept.
  • Charts, diagrams, maps and music scores: a chart’s numbers open as a table beside it, but a chart with several series tells them apart by colour unless your system asks for more contrast. A flowchart’s picture lists its words without saying how they connect, and nothing describes it. A map is named only “Map”, and nothing gives its places in text. A score’s notes open as text, in a notation for reading music.
  • Answers and tables: tables in answers and documents, and a collection’s rows, don’t mark their row heads. Tables in answers, and the homepage’s, aren’t tied to the line that titles them. A document’s passages in other languages aren’t marked as such. A short answer in another language can be marked as the page’s language, and Jean’s own words inside an answer in another language aren’t marked.
  • Code: on pages in Arabic, the wordmark relies on automatic direction.
  • Files you save: we read how Word documents and Excel workbooks are built, but haven’t yet run Word’s and Excel’s own accessibility checkers, as RGAA asks. Word documents have real headings, lists, table headers and their language, but no title yet. Workbooks have a named sheet and a frozen first row, not a declared table.

What comes next

We’re working through the list above. Each time readers who didn’t make the fixes judge them again, we publish the new share here. A person’s check with a screen reader is still to come.

What we test on every change

We aim for WCAG 2.2, level AA, which covers what the European standard, EN 301 549, asks of web content.

Beyond this audit, automated tests run on every change, on the first screen, a conversation, Settings and the tasks, and on every page of this site. They cover those pages only and they miss things, so passing them doesn’t make a page accessible:

  • Contrast of words: every word on those screens against the pixels behind it, in every look, by night and by day.
  • Contrast of controls: buttons that show only an icon, the frames of text boxes and focus rings, at 3:1 against what is beside them.
  • Zoom: at 400% those screens lose no words, and text at 200% holds too.
  • Targets: on those screens, everything a pointer can press is at least 24 by 24 pixels, or spaced as if it were.
  • Motion: at rest nothing moves for more than five seconds, and with your system’s reduced motion nothing moves at all.
  • This site: each page is checked in a browser with axe-core for what WCAG 2.2 asks at levels A and AA.

You can read how each check is made.

How we audited

Who: Warehouse SAS itself, on 1 November 2026, with no person checking the judgments. Claude, Anthropic’s AI, made the tests and the first judgments for us. Separate AI readers, each given a part of the criteria and none of those judgments, then judged them again in three rounds, and the last verdict on each criterion counts. Of the 49 criteria met, 19 rest on the second read, 12 on the third and 18 on the fourth, which read a copy of Jean with our fixes and was told of none of them.

The sample: 49 pages and screens.

  • Asked for by RGAA: the homepage, the terms with their legal notice, this page, the site map, the help centre, and the lock screen, the only sign-in Jean has. There is no separate contact page.
  • The main uses: a help article, a task with an answer, search, the tasks, Settings’ General and Capabilities, Creations, a design, a slide deck, charts of one series and of several, a score, a request for approval, and the pages for IT, families, Excel, bringing your history and comparing with ChatGPT.
  • What you create: a document new and empty, in use, its menus, and with Jean’s changes to accept or reject; a collection new and empty, with Jean’s changes, its menus, and as a board.
  • What ordinary use produces: a flowchart, a map, and an answer with sources, each looked at for what it adds.
  • Every step of a process: connecting a first AI, setting a lock, and asking for a key.
  • A different look: a task in the Terminal and Mondrian looks, Settings’ Appearance, and the homepage in Arabic.
  • Files: an answer and a document saved as Word documents, and a collection saved as an Excel workbook.
  • Five chosen at random: the comparison with Open WebUI, the comparisons’ page, the help articles on what Jean costs and on starting free with OpenRouter, and the keyboard shortcuts.

Tools: axe-core 4.14, html-validate 9.7, W3C’s Nu Html Checker, Playwright 1.64, and Jean’s own tests of contrast, zoom, targets and motion, along with the page’s code, read by hand.

Test environment: Chromium 156 on macOS 27, by keyboard and pointer. RGAA’s tests of what assistive technology announces need Firefox with NVDA and Safari with VoiceOver: they weren’t run, so what depends on them isn’t counted as met.

Technologies: HTML, CSS, JavaScript and WAI-ARIA.

Tell us

If something in Jean stops you, if you need something it shows in another form, or if this page is wrong, write to arnaud@jean.is: what you were doing, the browser, and any assistive technology you use. We’ll say what we’ll change, and when.

If you get no answer

In France, you can turn to the Défenseur des droits, which protects people from discrimination, disability included: through its website, through one of its local delegates, or by post, with no stamp, at Défenseur des droits, Libre réponse 71120, 75342 Paris CEDEX 07.

If your organisation serves its own copy

This page speaks for jean.is, not for your copy. An organisation that must publish a statement, as public bodies in the EU do, and in France companies with over 250 million euros of turnover, publishes its own for the address it serves Jean from: what its policy turns off, its own contact, and its country’s procedure for complaints.

In France, that means a declaration on RGAA’s template, from an audit of your own copy, with your multi-year plan, this year’s action plan, and the homepage mention that audit earns. Our grid can help prepare that audit, but it doesn’t replace it.
