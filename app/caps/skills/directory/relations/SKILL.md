# Who's who

Find the people, organisations, projects, places and documents in what the user shares, and how they are linked. Keep them as a spreadsheet with `write`, and draw them as a map in the answer.

## Steps

1. Work from what the user names or shares: files, the conversation's folder, an earlier task, notes, or one search of a connected service (their email, their drive) for what they asked about. Never go through everything they have. When it isn't clear what to read, ask with `ask_user`.
2. Read it all before drawing. For each source, note who and what it names: people, organisations, projects, places and documents, with dates when they matter. Note how they are linked, in a few words in the user's language ("works for", "sent", "signed", "is about", "owes"), each link with the source it comes from.
3. Keep one entry for each person or thing. Two names are the same person when they share an email address, or a full name and an organisation; say which ones you merged, and ask with `ask_user` rather than guess when you aren't sure. Never invent a link: what the sources don't say isn't on the map.
4. Make a spreadsheet with `write`, named in the user's language, such as "Who's who - Dupont sale.xlsx", with two sheets as Markdown tables, each under a heading that names its sheet:
   - "People and things": Name, Kind (person, organisation, project, place, document), Role or note, Sources;
   - "Links": From, Link, To, Source, each From and To a name from the first sheet.
   When the user shares that spreadsheet again or asks for a change, start from it, since they may have corrected it, and change it with `edit` rather than starting over.
5. In the answer, draw the map in a fenced block tagged `echarts`, an ECharts option in strict JSON:
   - one series with `"type": "graph"`, `"layout": "force"`, `"roam": true`, `"draggable": true`, `"force": { "repulsion": 300, "edgeLength": 120 }`, `"edgeSymbol": ["none", "arrow"]`, `"label": { "show": true, "position": "right" }` and `"edgeLabel": { "show": true, "formatter": "{c}" }`;
   - `categories`, one for each kind, named in the user's language, with a `legend` of them and a `tooltip`;
   - `data`: each person or thing as `{ "name": "…", "category": 0, "symbolSize": 30 }`, its category the index of its kind, its size between 20 and 60, larger for those with more links;
   - `links`: each as `{ "source": "…", "target": "…", "value": "…" }`, the names of the two ends and the link's words;
   - sixty people and things at most: past that, keep those with the most links and say what was left out. No colours: they follow the user's theme.
6. Then say in a few sentences what the map shows: who is at the centre, which groups there are, which links rest on a single source, and what seems missing or contradicts itself, citing the sources.
7. Answer later questions ("who knows both of them?", "what did the bank send?") from the spreadsheet and the sources, citing them. When the user wants the map kept up to date, such as each week from their email, suggest saving these steps as a flow, which can run on a schedule.
